CASE-01 · Incident response & vulnerability remediation SOCVuln Mgmt Kali · Nessus · Velociraptor

Nessus triage and a Linux persistence hunt

Week 4 of the vulnerability management capstone: turned a Nessus scan into tracked tickets, ranked the medium findings, then investigated a Kali host with Velociraptor.

What I did

  • Fixed T-6 by enabling the EnableCertPaddingCheck registry value, then rescanned to confirm zero critical and zero high findings on that host.
  • For the end-of-life server, documented why patching was impossible and proposed isolation, strict firewall rules, disabled services and SIEM/EDR monitoring until the workload migrates.
  • Recorded a resolution timeline from scan discovery and noted that a real SOC needs automatic ticket timestamps to measure MTTR.
  • Planned next steps for the failed credentialed scan: check scanner permissions, SSH and firewall rules, Nessus logs, and fall back to an uncredentialed scan for partial visibility.

Top 5 medium findings, by priority

  1. MS13-036 Four kernel CVEs (race conditions, font parsing, NTFS null deref) lead to kernel privilege escalation.
  2. MS15-063 DLL planting via LoadLibrary grants full admin, but needs plant + trigger.
  3. MS12-033 Partition Manager EoP; serious but less documented.
  4. MS13-077 Service Control Manager EoP; vague advisory, no clear exploit chain.
  5. MS14-019 Needs a user to run a crafted .bat/.cmd and gives no privilege gain.

Host investigation findings

Root crontab persistence

@reboot /usr/sbin/shutdown +360 in root's crontab, not part of a default Kali build. Recommended removal, a sweep of /etc/cron.d and all user crontabs, a review of auth.log, and rotating sudo credentials.

Anti-forensics in shell history

~/.zsh_history opened with sudo crontab -e followed by history -c: someone edited the scheduler and then cleared history to cover it.

Hidden file ruled out

Found .bashrc.original, a pattern used to hide injected payloads. Diffed it against the live .bashrc: only cosmetic aliases and prompt changes.

No rogue accounts

Manual review of /etc/passwd (UID ≥ 1000) and /etc/shadow, cross-checked with Velociraptor's Linux.Sys.Users and Linux.Sys.LastUserLogin artifacts.

CASE-02 · Validate, exploit, remediate Vuln MgmtSOC Nessus · Metasploit · Windows Firewall · 2026

EternalBlue on Windows 7: from scan finding to blocked attack

Proved a Nessus finding was real by exploiting it, then remediated an end-of-life host that can't be patched and rescanned to confirm.

  • Find: ran a Nessus Basic Network Scan against the Windows 7 VM and confirmed MS17-010 (EternalBlue), then broke down the CVSS metrics of its CVEs.
  • Validate: exploited it from Kali with Metasploit's ms17_010_eternalblue module, got a Meterpreter session, migrated into explorer.exe to hide the process, and captured the victim's keystrokes with the keylogger.
  • Remediate: turning on the firewall alone changed nothing in the rescan. I then created inbound rules blocking TCP 445 and 139 on every profile, and the next rescan no longer showed the SMB findings.
  • Next steps: identified more ports to close from the remaining Criticals, port 21 (FileZilla FTP) and 3389 (RDP).

Finding

What would have stopped the breach? A CIS Controls gap analysis

A case study breach: an unregistered personal laptop on corporate Wi-Fi carried a worm that reached an anonymous-access FTP server, and a large encrypted exfiltration of proprietary designs was routed to the help desk instead of security.

For each gap I explained how the safeguard would have broken the attack chain. For example, network access control would have dropped the laptop onto a guest VLAN that couldn't reach the FTP server, and a severity matrix would have sent a large outbound transfer straight to the security team.

CASE-04 · Incident reporting & email analysis SOCGRC OWASP · FERPA · Thunderbird

Writing up a pen-test finding for leadership

An incident report, written as the information security manager, after a third-party penetration test found broken access control and injection flaws in a university's student portal.

  • Documented discovery, validation, scope and duration: an estimated 22,000 students and 80 staff exposed for about six months.
  • Assessed impact on data, systems, money, reputation and regulation (FERPA, GDPR), and classified the information involved by sensitivity.
  • Built a mitigation plan with timelines: WAF virtual patches and IDS within a month, stored procedures, deny-by-default access, server-side authorization checks, and a possible rollback of the faulty update.

Also: email header analysis
In a separate lab I read a message's full headers in Thunderbird and traced each hop between the two mail servers, the virus-scan stamps, the DKIM signature and the message ID, the same skills used to triage phishing.
